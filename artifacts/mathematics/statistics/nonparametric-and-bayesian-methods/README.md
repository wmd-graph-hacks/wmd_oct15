# Nonparametric and Bayesian Methods

Nonparametric procedures make fewer assumptions about a particular distributional form. Rank tests replace raw values by order; permutation tests compare observed statistics with values generated by rearrangements justified by the design or null hypothesis. “Nonparametric” does not mean assumption-free: exchangeability, independence, and measurement conditions still matter.

Bayesian inference combines a prior distribution with a likelihood to form a posterior: p(θ|data)∝p(data|θ)p(θ). The prior represents information before the data in the model; the likelihood represents data compatibility at each parameter value. A posterior supports probability statements about parameters conditional on model and prior assumptions.

**Example.** Let p be the probability of success and choose a Beta(1,1) uniform prior. After s successes and f failures in Bernoulli trials, posterior is Beta(1+s,1+f). With 7 successes and 3 failures it is Beta(8,4), posterior mean 8/12=2/3. A different prior changes the result, especially with sparse data; report sensitivity when relevant.

Frequentist and Bayesian intervals answer different questions. A confidence interval has repeated-procedure coverage; a credible interval contains a specified posterior probability. Neither framework removes the need to assess data quality and model fit.

## Lesson: rank methods and Bayesian updating

Nonparametric procedures make fewer distribution-shape assumptions than many classical tests. Rank tests replace raw values by order information and can handle skewed data or outliers, though they answer questions about distributions/ranks and are not automatically tests of medians. Examples include sign and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for paired data, Mann–Whitney for two independent groups, and Spearman rank correlation.

Bayesian inference combines a prior distribution for a parameter with a likelihood from observed data. The posterior is proportional to prior×likelihood and is normalized to integrate or sum to 1. A prior expresses information before the current data; posterior conclusions depend on both model and prior, especially with small samples. Credible intervals have a direct posterior probability interpretation conditional on those choices.

**Worked example.** Let p be a coin’s head probability with uniform prior Beta(1,1). After 8 heads in 10 tosses, the posterior is Beta(9,3), because successes and failures update the two parameters. Its posterior mean is 9/12=0.75. The estimate incorporates the prior’s two pseudo-counts; with much more data, the likelihood dominates.

**Practice with answers.** (1) Paired before/after measurements suggest a paired rank procedure, not an independent-samples test. (2) A 95% credible interval contains 95% posterior probability under the model. (3) A p-value is not a posterior probability. (4) State prior, likelihood, and data to make a Bayesian result interpretable.

**Common pitfalls:** treating a nonparametric test as assumption-free; choosing tests without respecting paired/group structure; and presenting a Bayesian posterior without stating its prior/model.
