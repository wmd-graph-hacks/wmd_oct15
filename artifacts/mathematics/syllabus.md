# Mathematics syllabus for Grades 11–12

This syllabus is a two-year learning plan built from the broader Mathematics resource graph in this folder. It is designed for students who have completed secondary-school algebra, coordinate geometry, and elementary statistics. The course builds calculation fluency and mathematical communication together. Proof-heavy undergraduate subjects in the graph are extension material; they are not prerequisites for completing the school-level pathway.

## Course outcomes

By the end of Grade 12, students should be able to:

1. Translate statements between words, symbols, tables, graphs, and diagrams; use sets, functions, and quantified statements accurately.
2. Construct short direct, contrapositive, contradiction, and induction proofs, and distinguish a valid argument from a plausible example.
3. Analyze functions and sequences; evaluate limits, differentiate and integrate standard functions, and explain derivative and integral results in context.
4. Solve problems involving vectors, matrices, systems of linear equations, and introductory eigenvalue ideas.
5. Count finite outcomes, model conditional probability, summarize data, and interpret uncertainty without confusing association with causation.
6. Formulate a mathematical model, state its assumptions, select a method, check units and reasonableness, and communicate a complete solution.

## Entry knowledge and diagnostic

Students should be comfortable with signed numbers, fractions, ratios, exponents, factorization, linear and quadratic equations, coordinate graphs, angle measure in radians, and basic trigonometric functions. In Week 1, use a short diagnostic with no grading penalty: simplify expressions, solve equations, read a graph, use function notation, and explain one reasoning step. Revisit gaps in a parallel algebra clinic rather than delaying the whole class.

## Two-year sequence

Each year assumes approximately 36 teaching weeks. A suggested rhythm is 8 weeks of instruction, 1 week of consolidation, repeated four times, with flexibility for local holidays and examinations. The listed weeks are approximate; mastery evidence determines whether a class moves ahead.

### Grade 11: functions, reasoning, and foundations for calculus

| Weeks | Unit | Essential learning | Suggested evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Algebra and function readiness | Domains, range, composition, inverse functions, transformations, polynomial and rational behavior, exponential and logarithmic laws, radians and unit-circle values | Interpret a function from formula, graph, and context; diagnose an invalid algebraic step |
| 3–5 | Mathematical language and proof | Propositions, implications, quantifiers, sets, relations, functions; direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, counterexample, induction | Write a proof of a parity/divisibility claim and negate a quantified statement correctly |
| 6–9 | Sequences and counting | Arithmetic/geometric sequences, sigma notation, permutations, combinations, binomial theorem, recurrence patterns | Derive a counting model and justify whether order or repetition matters |
| 10–13 | Limits and continuity | Numerical and graphical limits, one-sided limits, algebraic techniques, continuity, Intermediate Value Theorem; intuitive epsilon-delta meaning | Explain a removable discontinuity and use IVT to prove a root exists |
| 14–18 | Derivatives | Difference quotient, derivative rules, chain rule, implicit differentiation, tangent and rate interpretations | Find and interpret a rate of change with units; estimate from a graph and compare with an exact derivative |
| 19–21 | Derivative applications | Critical points, monotonicity, concavity, curve sketching, optimization, related rates; Mean Value Theorem as a guarantee | Solve an optimization problem, state the domain, and verify endpoints and critical points |
| 22–25 | Integral ideas | Antiderivatives, Riemann sums, definite integral as signed accumulation, Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, area and average value | Approximate an integral from a table, then evaluate and interpret it using the FTC |
| 26–29 | Integration methods | Substitution; introductory integration by parts and trigonometric methods as enrichment; area between curves and volumes where suitable | Choose and explain a technique; check an antiderivative by differentiation |
| 30–32 | Probability and data | Sample spaces, counting-based probability, conditional probability, independence, Bayes’ rule, descriptive statistics and sampling | Build a probability tree and explain a base-rate result in words |
| 33–36 | Synthesis and project | Mixed calculus review; mathematical modeling project; reflection and targeted reassessment | Portfolio: one proof, one calculus model, one probability/data investigation |

### Grade 12: integration, vectors, and mathematical modeling

| Weeks | Unit | Essential learning | Suggested evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Retrieval and series | Revisit limits and derivatives; sequences, convergence intuition, geometric series, power series and Taylor polynomials at an appropriate level | Approximate a function near a point and describe the interval/conditions where the approximation is useful |
| 4–7 | Definite and improper integration | Accumulation, substitution, integration by parts, partial fractions for suitable rational functions, improper integrals and convergence | Distinguish finite area from a convergent improper integral; justify a numerical or exact answer |
| 8–11 | Vectors and analytic geometry | Vectors in 2D/3D, dot product, projections, lines and planes, distances and angles; vector-valued motion | Find a projection and explain its geometric meaning; model position, velocity, and acceleration |
| 12–16 | Matrices and linear systems | Matrix operations, Gaussian elimination, determinants as an invertibility test, linear transformations, span, independence, basis and dimension in concrete spaces | Solve and interpret a system; explain pivot variables and free variables |
| 17–19 | Eigenvalues and applications | Eigenvectors, eigenvalues, diagonalization for simple matrices, repeated iteration and stability intuition | Verify an eigenpair and use it to describe repeated application of a transformation |
| 20–23 | Multivariable introduction | Surfaces and level sets, partial derivatives, gradient, chain rule, directional rates, constrained optimization intuition | Use a gradient to identify steepest increase and solve a two-variable optimization problem |
| 24–27 | Probability and statistical inference | Random variables, expectation and variance, common discrete/continuous distributions, sampling variability, confidence intervals and hypothesis-test interpretation | Interpret an interval and p-value in context; identify the population, sample, and assumptions |
| 28–30 | Discrete mathematics and networks | Inclusion-exclusion, recurrences, graph representations, paths, trees, connectivity and simple network questions | Prove a small graph claim or compare two counting approaches |
| 31–33 | Numerical methods and computation | Rounding and error, bisection/Newton ideas, numerical integration, Euler/Runge–Kutta overview, conditioning | Compare an approximation with an exact value or known bound and explain error sources |
| 34–36 | Capstone and transition | Integrative project; proof and modeling review; optional preview of analysis, algebra, ODEs, or number theory | Present a written report with question, assumptions, method, result, limitations, and references to the lessons used |

## Core, enrichment, and advanced extension

**Core for most Grade 11–12 learners:** algebra/function readiness; logic and introductory proofs; limits and continuity; derivatives and applications; definite integrals and standard integration techniques; sequences and geometric series; vectors and matrices; counting and probability; descriptive statistics and introductory inference.

**Enrichment when core fluency is secure:** epsilon-delta proofs, full Mean Value Theorem proofs, Taylor remainder/error estimates, integration by parts and partial fractions, eigenvalue applications, multivariable gradients and Lagrange multipliers, simple numerical algorithms, and graph theory.

**Later undergraduate extension:** full multivariable integral theorems; rigorous real analysis and Lebesgue integration; abstract algebra and Galois theory; complex analysis; systems of ODEs and PDEs; advanced probability and statistics; convex optimization and KKT conditions; topology and quadratic reciprocity. These remain available as linked study materials, but should not displace the Grade 11–12 core unless the learner has the relevant prerequisites and time.

## Instructional cycle for each subtopic

Plan a 50–70 minute lesson (or split it over two periods):

1. **Retrieve (5–8 min):** ask two questions from earlier lessons and one prerequisite check. Students answer from memory before discussion.
2. **Define (8–12 min):** introduce the objects and notation; connect each definition to one example and one non-example.
3. **Model (10–15 min):** work one representative problem aloud. Name the decision points, assumptions, and checks, not just the algebraic steps.
4. **Guide (12–18 min):** solve a near-transfer problem with students; remove prompts gradually.
5. **Practice (10–15 min):** assign one routine item, one explanation/proof item, and one changed-context problem.
6. **Exit reflection (3–5 min):** students state the key idea, one likely mistake, and one question. Use responses to plan review.

Space retrieval of definitions, theorem conditions, and representative methods after about 1, 3, 7, and 21 days. Keep an error log with the original reasoning, the corrected idea, and a fresh problem that tests the correction.

## Assessment and feedback

- **Diagnostic:** low-stakes readiness check; use it to plan support, never as a fixed label.
- **Weekly formative checks:** short retrieval, one worked calculation, and one explanation or proof. Return feedback while the lesson is still active.
- **Unit assessment:** balance conceptual interpretation (25%), procedural accuracy (35%), reasoning/proof (20%), and modeling/transfer (20%). Adjust weight when a unit has a different purpose.
- **Cumulative assessment:** mix spaced questions from earlier units with current work; provide a formula sheet only when recall is not the learning target.
- **Corrections and reassessment:** require students to explain the source of an error and solve a parallel problem. Record what they can do now rather than averaging repeated attempts mechanically.
- **Mastery signal:** look for accurate, independent performance on varied questions at more than one time point, plus an explanation of assumptions. A single score is evidence, not a complete diagnosis.

## Differentiation

For learners needing support, use worked-example pairs, visual representations, prerequisite mini-lessons, vocabulary cards, and fewer but carefully chosen practice questions. For learners ready to extend, ask for a proof, a counterexample, a second solution method, a parameter change, or a numerical experiment. Keep the same central mathematical idea so that students can discuss their approaches together.
